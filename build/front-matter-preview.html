Inside the STAAR Grade 8 Test

The Final STAAR Year

STAAR — the State of Texas Assessments of Academic Readiness — measures how well students have learned the Texas Essential Knowledge and Skills (TEKS) for their grade. Under Texas House Bill 8 (2025), spring 2027 is the last STAAR administration for grades 3–8. A new assessment called the Student Success Tool is scheduled to replace it in 2027–2028. This book prepares you fully for that final STAAR year.

A Computer-Based Test

STAAR is given online, on a computer. The real test includes question types beyond simple multiple choice — you might type an answer, choose from a drop-down menu, drag items into place, or build an equation. A paper book can't make you click and drag, so this book does the next best thing: it shows every online question type on paper and teaches you exactly how to answer it. The thinking is identical; only the clicking is different.

How Much Time?

STAAR for grades 3–8 is given with a generous time window of up to four hours. It is not a speed test — most students finish comfortably. Still, practicing with a timer builds a steady, calm pace.

Watch Out: Written responses carry real points — up to 14 of the 56 RLA points come from the SCRs and ECR. Never leave them blank. A partial, organized response almost always earns more than nothing.

How STAAR Is Scored

From Raw Points to a Performance Level

Each question earns raw points (1 or 2 for most; the Extended Constructed Response is worth 10). Your total raw points convert to a scale score, which places you in one of four performance levels:

Performance LevelWhat It Means
Masters Grade LevelStrong command of the grade's content; ready for the next grade with little support.
Meets Grade LevelSolid understanding; likely to succeed next grade with some support.
Approaches Grade LevelSome understanding; likely needs targeted support next grade.
Did Not Meet Grade LevelNot enough understanding yet; needs significant support.

The goal for most students is Meets Grade Level or higher — and this book is built to get you there.

How Written Responses Are Scored

  • Short Constructed Response (SCR) — a few sentences answering a specific question, scored 0 to 2 points. Earn full credit by making a clear point and backing it with correct evidence from the text.
  • Extended Constructed Response (ECR) — a full multi-paragraph essay, scored 0 to 10 points. The rubric rewards a clear central idea or claim, organized paragraphs, specific supporting evidence, and correct language and conventions.

You will learn to earn these points step by step in the SCR Mastery and ECR Mastery sections, which include real-style prompts and sample responses scored at high, middle, and low levels.

Tracking Your Own Scores

After each practice test, use the Score-Tracking Sheets and Strand Diagnostic Charts at the back. They turn raw points into a performance estimate and show you which strand or skill to study next. Watching your scores climb from Test 1 to Test 4 is the clearest sign your work is paying off.

Item-Type Field Guide

How Online Questions Look on Paper

STAAR is taken on a computer, so some questions ask you to do more than bubble a letter. This guide shows every question type you may meet, how we present it on paper in this book, and a quick example. The skill tested is always a Grade 8 TEKS skill — only the way you record your answer changes.

Key Idea: On screen you click, type, or drag. On paper you circle, write, or draw an arrow. The thinking is exactly the same.

1. Multiple Choice

Online: Click one choice (A–D). On paper: Circle the letter of the one best answer.

Example (Math). Which number is the greatest?   A. 0.6   B. ⅗   C. 0.58   D. ½   (Answer: A)

2. Multiselect

Online: Check every correct box. On paper: Circle all correct letters; the question says how many.

Example (Math). Select the two numbers that are irrational.   A. √49   B. √10   C. π   D. 0.25   (Answers: B and C)

3. Inline Choice (Drop-Down)

Online: Click a drop-down inside the sentence. On paper: Each drop-down is a box of choices; circle the correct one.

Example (Revising & Editing). The science club meets after school, and the members is / are / was planning a fundraiser. (Answer: are)

4. Text Entry / Griddable

Online: Type a number or short answer; for numbers you may fill a grid. On paper: Write in the box, then bubble the grid when provided.

Example (Math). A line passes through (0, 3) with slope 2. What is y when x = 4?       (Answer: 11)

5. Drag-and-Drop

Online: Drag words/numbers from a bank into blanks or an order. On paper: A bank is printed; write each item on the correct line or draw an arrow.

Example (Math). Order from least to greatest. Bank: 0.4, ⅓, 0.37, ⅖   Least __ __ __ __ Greatest   (Answer: ⅓, 0.37, 0.4, ⅖)

6. Equation Editor

Online: Build an expression/equation with an on-screen keypad. On paper: Write it neatly on the line.

Example (Math). Write an equation in form y = mx + b for slope 3, y-intercept −5.       (Answer: y = 3x − 5)

7. Graphing

Online: Plot points, lines, or bars on a grid. On paper: Use the printed coordinate grid and a pencil.

Example (Math). On the grid provided, plot (−2, 4) and draw the line y = x + 1.

8. Hot Text

Online: Click a sentence or phrase in the passage. On paper: Underline, or write the number of, the sentence that answers the question.

Example (Reading). Underline the sentence that best supports the idea that the narrator felt nervous.

9. Written Responses (SCR and ECR)

Online: Type in a text box. On paper: Write in the lined space provided.

  • Short Constructed Response (SCR) — a few sentences with text evidence (2 points).
  • Extended Constructed Response (ECR) — a full multi-paragraph essay (10 points).

You will practice these in depth in the SCR Mastery and ECR Mastery sections.

Strategy: When a question says "Select TWO" or "least to greatest," underline those words before you answer. Many points are lost not from wrong math or reading, but from missing the directions.

Test-Day Game Plan

You have practiced the skills and the question types. Test day is simply one more practice test — this time for real. Follow this plan to stay calm and finish strong.

The Night Before

  • Pack what you need and go to bed on time. Rest helps your brain more than last-minute cramming.
  • Do a light review of your diagnostic charts only — no new material.

The Morning Of

  • Eat a good breakfast. Arrive early so you feel settled, not rushed.
  • Take a few slow breaths before you begin. You are ready.

During the Test — Your Pacing Plan

  1. Read the directions of each question carefully. Watch for select two, least to greatest, and best.
  2. Answer the questions you know first. Flag harder ones and return — don't let one question eat your time.
  3. Use your tools. On Math, use the calculator and reference sheet and show work on scratch paper. On Reading, use the online dictionary when a word blocks understanding.
  4. Eliminate wrong answers. Crossing out two choices doubles your odds on a tricky question.
  5. Never leave a question blank. There is no penalty for guessing. Make your best choice and flag it to revisit.

Budgeting Time for Written Responses (RLA)

  • Keep SCR answers short and direct — make your point, give text evidence, done.
  • Save time for the ECR essay (10 points). A quick plan — thesis, two or three supporting points with evidence, a conclusion — keeps you organized. A partial, organized essay still earns points; a blank one earns none.

Before You Submit

  • Return to every flagged question; make sure each has an answer.
  • Check that multiselect questions have the right number of choices marked.
  • Reread written responses once for clear sentences, correct spelling, and punctuation.
Key Idea: Finish every question with some answer, give the written responses real effort, and use your full time. Steady and complete beats fast and blank.

Strand 1 · Numerical Representations and Relationships

What this strand tests: how to represent, compare, and order real numbers — including irrational numbers and scientific notation.

Standards covered:

  • ○ 8.2A — Describe relationships between sets of real numbers using a visual representation.
  • ○ 8.2B — Approximate an irrational number (including π and square roots of numbers less than 225) and locate it on a number line.
  • ○ 8.2C — Convert between standard decimal notation and scientific notation.
  • ★ 8.2D — Order a set of real numbers from mathematical and real-world contexts. (Readiness)

8.2A · The Real Number System  ○

Every number you use at Grade 8 is a real number. Real numbers divide into groups nested inside one another:

  • Natural: 1, 2, 3, …   Whole: 0, 1, 2, 3, …   Integers: … −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, …
  • Rational: any number written as a fraction a⁄b — integers, terminating decimals (0.75), repeating decimals (0.333…).
  • Irrational: cannot be written as a simple fraction; decimals never end or repeat — such as π and √2.
Key Idea: The groups are nested — every natural number is also whole, an integer, and rational. But rational and irrational never overlap.

Worked example. Classify √16 and √20.   √16 = 4 → natural, whole, integer, rational.   √20 ≈ 4.472… → irrational.

Watch Out: A square root is irrational only when the number is not a perfect square. √25 = 5 is rational; √26 is irrational.

Try It. Every integer is a rational number — true or false? (True: any integer n = n⁄1.)

8.2B · Approximating Irrational Numbers  ○

To estimate a square root, find the two perfect squares it falls between.

Worked example. Approximate √50.   49 = 72 and 64 = 82, so √50 is between 7 and 8; just above 49, so √50 ≈ 7.1 — mark it just right of 7 on a number line.   π ≈ 3.14, between 3 and 4.

Watch Out: "Less than 225" means the number under the root is below 225 — roots up to about √224 ≈ 14.97.

Try It. Between which two whole numbers is √30? (5 and 6, since 25 < 30 < 36.)

8.2C · Scientific Notation  ○

Scientific notation writes a number as a × 10n, where 1 ≤ |a| < 10 and n is an integer. Large numbers → positive exponent; small numbers (less than 1) → negative exponent.

  • 4,500,000 = 4.5 × 106   ·   0.00032 = 3.2 × 10−4   ·   7.1 × 105 = 710,000

Watch Out: The factor a must be at least 1 and less than 10. Writing 45 × 105 is wrong — fix to 4.5 × 106.

Try It. Write 0.0067 in scientific notation. (6.7 × 10−3.)

8.2D · Ordering Real Numbers  ★ (Readiness)

Numbers often appear in different forms at once — fractions, decimals, percents, roots, scientific notation. Convert them all to decimals, then compare.

Worked example. Order from least to greatest: ⅗, 0.58, √0.36, 62%.
⅗ = 0.60 · 0.58 = 0.58 · √0.36 = 0.60 · 62% = 0.62 → 0.58, then ⅗ = √0.36, then 62%.

Key Idea: One common form — usually the decimal — lets you compare very different-looking numbers fairly. A number line helps you see the order.

Watch Out: On a negative number line, the number farther left is smaller. −0.8 < −0.3.

Try It. Order greatest to least: 0.7, ⅔, 71%. (71% = 0.71, 0.7, ⅔ ≈ 0.667.)

Strand 2 · Computations and Algebraic Relationships

This is the largest strand on the test (about 15 of 40 questions). It is all about lines, relationships, and equations. Master it and you master most of the Math test.

Standards covered:

  • ○ 8.4A — Similar right triangles show slope is the same between any two points on a line.
  • ★ 8.4B — Graph proportional relationships; interpret the unit rate as the slope.
  • ★ 8.4C — Use a table or graph to find the rate of change (slope) and the y-intercept.
  • ○ 8.5A — Linear proportional situations as y = kx.   ○ 8.5B — Non-proportional as y = mx + b (b ≠ 0).
  • ○ 8.5E — Direct variation.   ○ 8.5F — Distinguish proportional from non-proportional.   ○ 8.5H — Examples of each.
  • ★ 8.5G — Identify functions from ordered pairs, tables, mappings, graphs.
  • ★ 8.5I — Write an equation y = mx + b to model a linear relationship.
  • ○ 8.8A — Write one-variable equations/inequalities with variables on both sides.   ○ 8.8B — Write a real-world problem for a given equation.   ★ 8.8C — Model and solve them.
  • ○ 8.9A — Identify a system's solution from the intersection of two graphs.

Part A · Slope and Rate of Change  (8.4A ○, 8.4B ★, 8.4C ★)

Slope measures how steep a line is — how much y changes for each change in x:

slope = m = (change in y) ÷ (change in x) = (y2 − y1) / (x2 − x1)

  • 8.4A — Why slope is constant. Right triangles drawn to any two points on the same line are similar, so rise ÷ run is the same everywhere — that ratio is the slope.
  • 8.4B — Proportional graphs. A proportional relationship passes through the origin (0, 0); equation y = kx; the unit rate is the slope k.
  • 8.4C — From a table or graph. Slope = (change in y) ÷ (change in x); the y-intercept is the y-value when x = 0.

Worked example (8.4C). Table: (0, 2), (1, 5), (2, 8). Slope = (5 − 2) ÷ (1 − 0) = 3; y-intercept = 2; equation y = 3x + 2.

Watch Out: Rise over run — count the vertical change first, then horizontal.

Try It. A line through (0, 0) and (4, 10) — slope? (10 ÷ 4 = 2.5.)

Part B · Proportional vs. Non-Proportional  (8.5A/B/E/F/H ○)

ProportionalNon-Proportional
Equationy = kxy = mx + b (b ≠ 0)
GraphLine through the originLine not through the origin
TableConstant ratio y⁄x = ky⁄x is not constant
  • 8.5E — Direct variation is a proportional relationship y = kx. If y = 15 when x = 3, then k = 5, so y = 5x.
  • 8.5F / 8.5H — Telling them apart. Does the line pass through (0, 0)? Yes → proportional. A starting amount (y-intercept ≠ 0) → non-proportional.

Worked example (8.5B). A gym charges a $20 sign-up fee plus $5 per visit: y = 5x + 20. The $20 fee (b ≠ 0) makes it non-proportional.

Watch Out: A per-item rate with no starting fee is proportional; add any flat fee and it becomes non-proportional.

Try It. Is y = 7x proportional? (Yes — passes through the origin, b = 0.)

Part C · Functions  (8.5G ★)

A function pairs each input (x) with exactly one output (y). In tables/mappings, an x-value may not repeat with two different y-values. On graphs, use the vertical line test — if any vertical line crosses the graph more than once, it is not a function.

Worked example. Is {(1, 3), (2, 5), (1, 7)} a function? No — input 1 pairs with both 3 and 7.

Try It. Does a non-vertical straight line pass the vertical line test? (Yes — it is a function.)

Part D · Writing Linear Equations  (8.5I ★)

To write y = mx + b, find the slope m and the y-intercept b.

Worked example. A plant is 4 cm tall and grows 2 cm each week. Slope m = 2; y-intercept b = 4; equation y = 2x + 4.

Strategy: "Starting amount" is almost always b; "rate per unit" is almost always m.

Try It. A taxi charges $3 to start plus $2 per mile. Equation for cost y after x miles? (y = 2x + 3.)

Part E · One-Variable Equations and Inequalities  (8.8A/B ○, 8.8C ★)

When the variable is on both sides, collect variable terms on one side and numbers on the other.

Worked example (8.8C). Solve 3x + 5 = x − 7.   Subtract x → 2x + 5 = −7   Subtract 5 → 2x = −12   Divide by 2 → x = −6.

8.8A write such equations from a situation; 8.8B do the reverse — describe a real situation the equation could model.

Watch Out: Whatever you do to one side, do to the other side — keep it balanced.

Try It. Solve 4x − 3 = 2x + 9. (2x = 12, x = 6.)

Part F · Systems of Two Linear Equations  (8.9A ○)

Two lines graphed together intersect at the point that satisfies both equations. That point (x, y) is the solution of the system.

Worked example. y = x + 1 and y = −x + 5 cross at (2, 3). Check: 3 = 2 + 1 ✓ and 3 = −2 + 5 ✓.

Try It. If two lines cross at (−1, 4), what is the solution? (x = −1, y = 4.)

Part A · Dilations and Similarity  (8.3A ○, 8.3B ○, 8.3C ★, 8.10D ○)

A dilation resizes a figure by a scale factor k from a center. With the origin as center:   (x, y) → (kx, ky).

  • 8.3A / 8.3B — Similar shapes. A figure and its dilation are similar: corresponding sides are proportional (×k) and angles stay the same.
  • 8.10D — Effect on measurements. Lengths multiply by k; area multiplies by k2.

Worked example (8.3C). Vertex (2, 3) dilated by factor 3 about the origin → (3·2, 3·3) = (6, 9).

Watch Out: k > 1 enlarges; 0 < k < 1 shrinks. Area changes by the square of k — factor 2 makes area 4×.

Try It. A square of side 5 is dilated by k = 2 — new side? (10.)

Part B · Transformations on the Coordinate Plane  (8.10A/B ○, 8.10C ★)

TransformationAlgebraic ruleChanges size?
Translation (slide)(x, y) → (x + a, y + b)No — congruent
Reflection over x-axis(x, y) → (x, −y)No — congruent
Reflection over y-axis(x, y) → (−x, y)No — congruent
Rotation 180° about origin(x, y) → (−x, −y)No — congruent
Rotation 90° counterclockwise(x, y) → (−y, x)No — congruent
Dilation (factor k)(x, y) → (kx, ky)Yes — similar, not congruent

8.10A / 8.10B — Congruence. Translations, reflections, and rotations are rigid — the image is congruent. A dilation (k ≠ 1) changes size, so it is not congruent, only similar.

Worked example (8.10C). Reflect (4, −2) over the x-axis → (4, 2).

Watch Out: Reflecting over the x-axis flips the y-sign; over the y-axis flips the x-sign.

Try It. Rotate (3, 5) by 180° about the origin. ((−3, −5).)

Part C · Angle Relationships  (8.8D ○)

  • Triangle angle sum: interior angles add to 180°.
  • Exterior angle = sum of the two remote interior angles.
  • Parallel lines + transversal: corresponding angles equal; alternate interior angles equal.
  • Angle-Angle (AA) similarity: two equal angles → triangles are similar.

Worked example. Angles 50° and 60° → third = 180 − 50 − 60 = 70°.

Try It. Angles 90° and 35° — find the third. (55°.)

Part D · Volume  (8.6A ○, 8.7A ★)

  • Cylinder: V = Bh = πr2h.   Cone: V = ⅓πr2h (one-third of the cylinder).   Sphere: V = 4⁄3 πr3.

Worked example (8.7A). Cylinder radius 3, height 10: V = π(32)(10) = 90π ≈ 282.6 cubic units.

Watch Out: The cone is ⅓ of the matching cylinder — don't forget the one-third.

Try It. Cone radius 3, height 10 — volume? (⅓·90π = 30π ≈ 94.2.)

Part E · Surface Area  (8.7B ★)

  • Prism — total: S = Ph + 2B.   Cylinder — total: S = 2πrh + 2πr2 (side plus two circles).

Worked example (8.7B). Cylinder radius 2, height 5: S = 2π(2)(5) + 2π(22) = 20π + 8π = 28π ≈ 87.9 square units.

Strategy: "Lateral" = the side only; "total" adds the bases. Read which one is asked.

Try It. Lateral surface area of that cylinder? (2πrh = 20π ≈ 62.8.)

Part F · The Pythagorean Theorem  (8.6C ○, 8.7C ★, 8.7D ○)

For a right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c:   a2 + b2 = c2.

  • 8.7C — Find a missing side, or use the converse to test whether a triangle is right.
  • 8.7D — Distance between two points: horizontal and vertical gaps are the legs; the distance is the hypotenuse.

Worked example (8.7C). Legs 6 and 8: c2 = 36 + 64 = 100 → c = 10.   (8.7D) Distance (1, 2) to (4, 6): legs 3 and 4 → √(9 + 16) = 5.

Watch Out: The hypotenuse c is across from the right angle and is the longest side.

Try It. Is 5, 12, 13 a right triangle? (Yes — 25 + 144 = 169 = 132.)
