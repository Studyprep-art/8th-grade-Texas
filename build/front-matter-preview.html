Welcome to Your STAAR Grade 8 Study Guide

Dear Student,

The STAAR Grade 8 tests in Reading Language Arts and Mathematics can feel like a lot — two subjects, many skills, a timed online screen, and even written essays. This book exists to make all of that feel familiar long before test day.

Here is the promise of this book: everything inside is on the test, and nothing on the test is left out. Every lesson, every practice question, and every writing prompt is tied directly to the Texas Essential Knowledge and Skills (TEKS) that STAAR measures at Grade 8. You will not waste a single minute on material that will not help you.

You will move through four stages:

  1. Learn — short, clear lessons that teach every tested skill.
  2. Practice — quick drills so each skill sticks.
  3. Simulate — eight full-length practice tests that look and feel like the real thing.
  4. Diagnose — answer explanations and charts that show you exactly what to review next.

Work at a steady pace, check every answer explanation (even for questions you got right), and use the diagnostic charts to spend your study time where it matters most. Confidence on test day comes from practice, and this book gives you plenty.

You can do this. Let's get started.

How to Use This Book

This book works for three kinds of readers. Find the path that fits you.

Path A — The Student Studying on Your Own

  1. Start with "Getting Started." Read how the test works, how it is scored, and how the online question types look on paper. About 30 minutes — and it saves you from surprises.
  2. Pick a study calendar. Use the 4-Week Sprint if the test is close, or the 8-Week Plan if you have more time.
  3. Learn, then practice. Read the skill lessons and do the warm-up drills before a full test.
  4. Take one full test at a time, under test-like conditions — quiet room, no phone, timed.
  5. Review every explanation, including for questions you answered correctly.
  6. Fill in your diagnostic chart after each test, then study your weakest strand next.

Path B — The Parent or Tutor Helping a Student

  • Use the "A Guide for Parents and Teachers" section at the back first.
  • Sit with the student for the first practice test to model pacing, then let them work more independently.
  • Focus praise on effort and strategy, not just the score. The tracking sheets make growth visible.

Path C — The Classroom Teacher

  • Lessons and drills map one-to-one to the TEKS; the TEKS Coverage Index lists where every standard is taught and practiced.
  • Each full-length test is blueprint-matched — usable as a benchmark or mock administration.
  • Explanations include the TEKS code and why each wrong choice is wrong, useful for reteaching.

Inside the STAAR Grade 8 Test

The Final STAAR Year

STAAR — the State of Texas Assessments of Academic Readiness — measures how well students have learned the Texas Essential Knowledge and Skills (TEKS) for their grade. Under Texas House Bill 8 (2025), spring 2027 is the last STAAR administration for grades 3–8. A new assessment called the Student Success Tool is scheduled to replace it in 2027–2028. This book prepares you fully for that final STAAR year.

A Computer-Based Test

STAAR is given online, on a computer. The real test includes question types beyond simple multiple choice — you might type an answer, choose from a drop-down menu, drag items into place, or build an equation. A paper book can't make you click and drag, so this book does the next best thing: it shows every online question type on paper and teaches you exactly how to answer it. The thinking is identical; only the clicking is different.

What Grade 8 Students Take

At Grade 8, students are assessed in Reading Language Arts, Mathematics, Science, and Social Studies. This book focuses on the two taken by every Grade 8 student every year: Reading Language Arts and Mathematics.

How Much Time?

STAAR for grades 3–8 is given with a generous time window of up to four hours. It is not a speed test — most students finish comfortably. Still, practicing with a timer builds a steady, calm pace.

Watch Out: Written responses carry real points — up to 14 of the 56 RLA points come from the SCRs and ECR. Never leave them blank. A partial, organized response almost always earns more than nothing.

How STAAR Is Scored

From Raw Points to a Performance Level

Each question earns raw points (1 or 2 for most; the Extended Constructed Response is worth 10). Your total raw points convert to a scale score, which places you in one of four performance levels:

Performance LevelWhat It Means
Masters Grade LevelStrong command of the grade's content; ready for the next grade with little support.
Meets Grade LevelSolid understanding; likely to succeed next grade with some support.
Approaches Grade LevelSome understanding; likely needs targeted support next grade.
Did Not Meet Grade LevelNot enough understanding yet; needs significant support.

The goal for most students is Meets Grade Level or higher — and this book is built to get you there.

How Written Responses Are Scored

  • Short Constructed Response (SCR) — a few sentences answering a specific question, scored 0 to 2 points. Earn full credit by making a clear point and backing it with correct evidence from the text.
  • Extended Constructed Response (ECR) — a full multi-paragraph essay, scored 0 to 10 points. The rubric rewards a clear central idea or claim, organized paragraphs, specific supporting evidence, and correct language and conventions.

You will learn to earn these points step by step in the SCR Mastery and ECR Mastery sections, which include real-style prompts and sample responses scored at high, middle, and low levels.

Tracking Your Own Scores

After each practice test, use the Score-Tracking Sheets and Strand Diagnostic Charts at the back. They turn raw points into a performance estimate and show you which strand or skill to study next. Watching your scores climb from Test 1 to Test 4 is the clearest sign your work is paying off.

Item-Type Field Guide

How Online Questions Look on Paper

STAAR is taken on a computer, so some questions ask you to do more than bubble a letter. This guide shows every question type you may meet, how we present it on paper in this book, and a quick example. The skill tested is always a Grade 8 TEKS skill — only the way you record your answer changes.

Key Idea: On screen you click, type, or drag. On paper you circle, write, or draw an arrow. The thinking is exactly the same.

1. Multiple Choice

Online: Click one choice (A–D). On paper: Circle the letter of the one best answer.

Example (Math). Which number is the greatest?   A. 0.6   B. ⅗   C. 0.58   D. ½   (Answer: A)

2. Multiselect

Online: Check every correct box. On paper: Circle all correct letters; the question says how many.

Example (Math). Select the two numbers that are irrational.   A. √49   B. √10   C. π   D. 0.25   (Answers: B and C)

3. Inline Choice (Drop-Down)

Online: Click a drop-down inside the sentence. On paper: Each drop-down is a box of choices; circle the correct one.

Example (Revising & Editing). The science club meets after school, and the members is / are / was planning a fundraiser. (Answer: are)

4. Text Entry / Griddable

Online: Type a number or short answer; for numbers you may fill a grid. On paper: Write in the box, then bubble the grid when provided.

Example (Math). A line passes through (0, 3) with slope 2. What is y when x = 4?       (Answer: 11)

5. Drag-and-Drop

Online: Drag words/numbers from a bank into blanks or an order. On paper: A bank is printed; write each item on the correct line or draw an arrow.

Example (Math). Order from least to greatest. Bank: 0.4, ⅓, 0.37, ⅖   Least __ __ __ __ Greatest   (Answer: ⅓, 0.37, 0.4, ⅖)

6. Equation Editor

Online: Build an expression/equation with an on-screen keypad. On paper: Write it neatly on the line.

Example (Math). Write an equation in form y = mx + b for slope 3, y-intercept −5.       (Answer: y = 3x − 5)

7. Graphing

Online: Plot points, lines, or bars on a grid. On paper: Use the printed coordinate grid and a pencil.

Example (Math). On the grid provided, plot (−2, 4) and draw the line y = x + 1.

8. Hot Text

Online: Click a sentence or phrase in the passage. On paper: Underline, or write the number of, the sentence that answers the question.

Example (Reading). Underline the sentence that best supports the idea that the narrator felt nervous.

9. Written Responses (SCR and ECR)

Online: Type in a text box. On paper: Write in the lined space provided.

  • Short Constructed Response (SCR) — a few sentences with text evidence (2 points).
  • Extended Constructed Response (ECR) — a full multi-paragraph essay (10 points).

You will practice these in depth in the SCR Mastery and ECR Mastery sections.

Strategy: When a question says "Select TWO" or "least to greatest," underline those words before you answer. Many points are lost not from wrong math or reading, but from missing the directions.

Test-Day Game Plan

You have practiced the skills and the question types. Test day is simply one more practice test — this time for real. Follow this plan to stay calm and finish strong.

The Night Before

  • Pack what you need and go to bed on time. Rest helps your brain more than last-minute cramming.
  • Do a light review of your diagnostic charts only — no new material.

The Morning Of

  • Eat a good breakfast. Arrive early so you feel settled, not rushed.
  • Take a few slow breaths before you begin. You are ready.

During the Test — Your Pacing Plan

  1. Read the directions of each question carefully. Watch for select two, least to greatest, and best.
  2. Answer the questions you know first. Flag harder ones and return — don't let one question eat your time.
  3. Use your tools. On Math, use the calculator and reference sheet and show work on scratch paper. On Reading, use the online dictionary when a word blocks understanding.
  4. Eliminate wrong answers. Crossing out two choices doubles your odds on a tricky question.
  5. Never leave a question blank. There is no penalty for guessing. Make your best choice and flag it to revisit.

Budgeting Time for Written Responses (RLA)

  • Keep SCR answers short and direct — make your point, give text evidence, done.
  • Save time for the ECR essay (10 points). A quick plan — thesis, two or three supporting points with evidence, a conclusion — keeps you organized. A partial, organized essay still earns points; a blank one earns none.

Before You Submit

  • Return to every flagged question; make sure each has an answer.
  • Check that multiselect questions have the right number of choices marked.
  • Reread written responses once for clear sentences, correct spelling, and punctuation.
Key Idea: Finish every question with some answer, give the written responses real effort, and use your full time. Steady and complete beats fast and blank.
